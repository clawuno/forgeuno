# Slide Roles and Layout Families

These are planning vocabularies, not mandatory slide types. A deck may omit any
role, combine compatible roles, or introduce another role when the story requires it.

## Narrative Roles

### Opening

Set the subject, stakes, and tone. Use a strong statement, image, or visual device;
avoid filling the cover with metadata that can appear elsewhere.

### Context and Tension

Explain what changed, why the issue matters, or which question the deck answers.
Good forms include a trend, before/after, annotated evidence, quote, or concise map
of the current situation.

### Evidence

Show the data, research, example, or mechanism that advances the argument. Give
the slide one evidence job and state the takeaway in the title when justified.

### Explanation

Make a complex relationship understandable with a process, system diagram,
comparison, sequence, decomposition, or annotated object.

### Proposal or Decision

Present the recommendation, options, tradeoffs, operating model, or requested
decision. Make the choice and its consequences visible rather than burying them
in bullets.

### Transition

Create a deliberate pause or change of scale. A transition does not require a
numbered divider; it may be a question, image, quote, or synthesis statement.

### Synthesis and Close

Consolidate what matters now, the decision, or the next action. Do not repeat the
deck verbatim. End with the audience's required takeaway.

### Reference and Appendix

Hold methods, detailed tables, sources, definitions, and backup evidence without
forcing projection copy onto the main narrative.

## Layout Families

Choose from relationships in the content, not from a desire for visual variety.

### Statement

One dominant sentence, number, quote, or conclusion with restrained support.
Useful for openings, transitions, synthesis, and high-confidence findings.

### Image-led

A full or partial bleed image with a clear text anchor. The image must carry
narrative meaning and have enough resolution for the crop.

### Evidence-led

A chart, table, screenshot, document excerpt, or diagram occupies the main visual
field; annotation and takeaway are secondary but explicit.

### Split

Two complementary fields such as evidence/explanation, before/after, or
problem/response. Avoid arbitrary 50/50 columns when one side deserves dominance.

### Comparison

Parallel structures for alternatives, segments, or tradeoffs. Keep axes and
levels aligned so the comparison is honest and scannable.

### Sequence

Steps, phases, timeline, journey, or causal chain. Direction and ordering must be
visually unambiguous; label relationships rather than relying on arrows alone.

### Matrix or Map

Position items by two meaningful dimensions, geography, hierarchy, or topology.
Explain axes and avoid treating arbitrary placement as data.

### Modular

Several related units with a clear reading order. Use only when the content is
genuinely modular; do not turn every idea into a card.

## Rhythm Planning

For each planned slide, record:

| Field | Question |
|-------|----------|
| Job | What changes for the audience after this slide? |
| Message | What is the single primary point? |
| Evidence | What makes the point credible? |
| Visual form | Chart, image, diagram, text, table, or mixed? |
| Layout family | Which spatial relationship fits the content? |
| Density | Spacious, standard, or evidence-dense? |

Review the plan as a sequence. Watch for repeated layout families, monotonous
density, a long run of text-heavy slides, or transitions that interrupt rather
than help the story.

## Content Limits

- If the title, body, and evidence all compete, the slide has no hierarchy.
- If body copy must become too small, edit, split, or move detail to the appendix.
- If a visual does not explain or prove anything, remove it.
- If a table must be shown, highlight the relevant row, column, or pattern rather
  than asking the audience to scan every cell.
